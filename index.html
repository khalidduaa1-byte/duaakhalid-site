01 / FIELD DATA SYSTEM

Beauty Advisor Sales Tracker

We already licensed a platform for this. It did not fit my market, so I built the replacement in two days and got 18 advisors onto it without anyone being told to.

READ THE CASE STUDY · RUN THE DEDUP +

PROBLEM

Egypt was my account and it was growing about 6x over 18 months. The advisors were sending me their daily sales over WhatsApp and I was retyping them into Excel every evening. That does not survive an account growing at that rate.

WHAT IT ACTUALLY WAS

The obvious answer was to put the market on the field sales platform we already licensed. It would not have helped. Loading a monthly target into it meant emailing the vendor's IT team and waiting, so the person responsible for the numbers could not change her own targets. The dashboard showed what the vendor had decided a dashboard shows. And the per seat cost could not be justified at that market size anyway. We did not have a missing tool. We had a tool nobody on my side could operate, and spending more on it would not have fixed that. Building the replacement cost nothing but my own time, so there was no spend to approve and no case to make. I built it.

BUILD

Two days from deciding to a running site, with Supabase behind it, which I learned on this project. The advisors are on their phones all day, so instead of shipping a native app I had them add the site to their home screen from the browser. Then I gave it to three advisors and the sales supervisor for a week before anyone else saw it. The supervisor got the dashboard with commissions and targets calculating themselves, which was the part she had been doing by hand. I took their feedback, fixed what they hated, and only then launched it to everyone. January to March I loaded myself by writing SQL against Supabase, because the app only went live in April.

PROOF

I had backfilled everything up to launch, and then advisors came online on different dates because of the signup problem. While somebody was still locked out I was entering their sales for them. When they got in they started logging their own, and they could not see what was already in there, so they re-entered days I had already covered. The dedup key is what decides whether two rows are one event or two. Here are two that look like two days of work and are actually one. The highlighted columns are the key. Run it.

ba_name entry_date store shift sales_amount status
Hana M. 2026-03-14 Cairo Morning 1,840 kept
Hana M. 2026-03-14 Cairo Morning 2,310 removed
ROWS2 SALES COUNTED4,150 DAYS WORKED2

The data columns and the four-column key are the real schema. Status is added here to show what the pipeline did, and the rule shown keeps the first occurrence. Values and advisor name are illustrative. The same export arrived with 38, 38, 40 and 37 columns across four consecutive months.

WHAT BROKE

The signup. Not the app, the signup. I had built the thing and tested it and it worked, and then advisors could not get in, because not everyone has an email they can actually access and not everyone has ever registered for anything. That is the part I did not see coming, and it had nothing to do with the product. So I kept WhatsApp running next to it for a month instead of forcing the change, and reconciled the two by hand until people were in.

IMPACT

One full run across January to April: 1,324 rows ingested, 0 rejected, 29 duplicates removed. Live with 18 advisors across Cairo, Sharm and Hurghada, none of whom were required to use it. I showed it to global beauty leadership about a month after it went out.

02 / AI PRODUCT · HACKATHON 2026

Homebase

A deterministic context block that helps a voice agent dispatch maintenance vendors. Structured by default, with the model behind an explicit opt-in flag.

READ THE CASE STUDY · TRY THE GENERATOR +

PROBLEM

Dispatching a maintenance vendor by phone means a human assembling the same context every time: what is broken, which unit, when the tenant is free, and what else that vendor already has booked. Assembled ad hoc, it comes out differently on every call and details get dropped.

WHAT IT ACTUALLY WAS

The obvious answer is to put a model on the call and let it write the script. But the variation was never the useful part. The inconsistency was the entire problem, and what a vendor needs on the other end of the phone is the same eight fields in the same order every time. That is a template problem, not a model problem. It is why the default path is a plain string builder and the model only runs behind a flag.

BUILD

Eight fixed fields, assembled per call so nothing is left to whoever is on the phone. The LLM path only runs with USE_LLM_SCOPE=true; the default is a plain template builder, because a string template was sufficient for the job.

PROOF

Change the ticket. The block keeps its shape, because the shape is the product.

build_context() · deterministic path

Same eight fields every call, whatever you pick. The system prompt never changes; only this block does.

WHAT BROKE

Most of it, in the sense that most of it never shipped. What exists after roughly three and a half hours is a CRUD app over six Firestore collections with seed data, no auth and test mode rules. The RERA compliance layer the README describes is listed as out of scope in that same README, so it is a specification and not a product, and I am not going to call it one. The concept page also advertises tenant satisfaction and response time figures for a build with no production usage. Those are not mine, they are not repeated here, and the screenshot above is cropped above them on purpose.

IMPACT

Hackathon build, co-founded, no production usage, so there is no impact number to claim and I am not inventing one. The decision worth showing instead: the voice agent's system prompt stays static while per-call variation is injected as a structured block, so the prompt is not rewritten on every call. The default path is a plain template, with the model only behind a flag, because a string template was sufficient.

03 / PERSONAL BUILD

Move-Out Sale Site & Tracker

One Python script generates the buyer-facing catalog and the internal profit tracker from a single item list, so the listing and the accounting cannot disagree.

READ THE CASE STUDY +

PROBLEM

Thirty one pieces of furniture to sell before I left the country, and every buyer asked the same three questions about size, condition and whether the price moved. One thread each, the same answers retyped.

WHAT IT ACTUALLY WAS

A catalog fixes the messaging. It does nothing about the part that was actually costing me money. I was pricing against store catalog pages rather than against what I had paid, and those pages were wrong by more than double. A dresser I had recorded at AED 1,100 had cost 499, so the site was advertising a saving that did not exist. The real problem was that the listing and the accounting were two separate documents, free to disagree with each other.

BUILD

The generator prices against purchase price, not the store catalog page, so a saving cannot be displayed unless it is real. Bundles carry their cost on the bundle line, with no invented per-item split, and unallocated cost stays visible instead of being quietly spread around.

PROOF

The live catalog, generated from the same item list that feeds the profit tracker.

WHAT BROKE

A dresser went live with a discount badge on it that was not real. I had it recorded at AED 1,100 when it actually cost 499, because I had trusted a store catalog page instead of the receipt. That is the whole reason the generator now prices against what was paid. The catalog also carried a counter that could read 0 items available instead of the real figure. I listed it on this site as an open defect before I fixed it, which I would rather do than have you find it. It is fixed now, and it reads 11.

IMPACT

20 of 31 items sold in 30 days. AED 8,400 recovered against AED 10,688 paid for those items, a 78.6% recovery rate. AED 4,900 still on the table across 11 items.

FIELD NOTES

Notes from the buying side.

For five years I was the customer for enterprise software in luxury retail, and most of what decided whether a tool actually got used had very little to do with the tool. These are the constraints I watched decide outcomes, what would have worked on me, and a log of one rollout from the inside.

01 / THE WALLS

Five things that decide whether a tool gets used.

None of these are unusual and none of them are anyone's fault. They are just what the buying side looks like from the inside. The second row of each is what I would want to know if I were on the other side of the table.

01 · AI IS BLOCKED ON THE LAPTOPS +

THE WALL

Across a lot of this industry, AI tools are blocked at the device level, for privacy and security reasons that are entirely real. The practical consequence is that the person closest to a problem is often the person least able to prototype anything against it. Worth knowing if you are selling in, because the colleague most likely to champion you is usually the one with the least access.

WHAT WOULD HAVE GOTTEN THROUGH

Nothing that starts by asking IT to unblock a domain. What gets through is a deployment that never needs install rights, or a pilot scoped so tightly that security is reviewing one workflow instead of a whole category of tool. Sell to the person whose numbers are wrong. Not to the person whose job is to say no.

02 · THE BUYER CANNOT CONFIGURE THE TOOL +

THE WALL

We already paid for a field sales platform. Loading a monthly target into it meant writing an email to the vendor's IT team and waiting for somebody there to do it. So the person who owned the account, whose targets they were, could not change her own targets. And the dashboard showed what the vendor had decided a dashboard should show, which was not what I needed to see.

WHAT WOULD HAVE GOTTEN THROUGH

Let the account owner change a routine setting without raising a ticket. Every hour of vendor side turnaround on something that simple is an hour in which somebody rebuilds the view they need in Excel. That is not a feature request. It is the difference between a tool that gets used and a spreadsheet that quietly replaces it.

03 · PER SEAT PRICING KILLS THE MARKETS THAT NEED IT +

THE WALL

The platform made sense across the large markets. Mine had a fraction of that headcount and a much lower cost base, so the per seat maths could not be justified for it. Which means the smallest markets, the ones with the least support and the most manual work, get the worst tooling. The place that needed it most was the place that could least afford it.

WHAT WOULD HAVE GOTTEN THROUGH

Price the small deployments like you actually want them. A market that size is not a rounding error, it is the pilot that proves the rollout, and it is where the person who would have championed you internally is currently doing the whole thing by hand at midnight.

04 · THE USER COULD NOT SIGN UP +

THE WALL

This is the one I got wrong myself, so it is the one I trust most. I built the tool, tested it, and it worked fine. Then the advisors could not get in. Registering needed an email address, and not everyone has an email account they can actually get into, and plenty of people have never signed up for anything online in their life. Nothing was broken. I had just never thought to check whether the people I built it for could reach the login screen.

WHAT WOULD HAVE GOTTEN THROUGH

Count the first screen as part of the product. If onboarding assumes an email, an app store account, an SSO identity or a password manager, you have already cut out a chunk of the field team you are being paid to reach. And do not force the cutover. I kept WhatsApp running alongside for a month and reconciled the two by hand, which was tedious, and which is also the only reason people came across at all.

05 · ANYTHING WITH A PRICE TAG MOVES SLOWLY +

THE WALL

Anything with a licence fee needs a business case, a procurement cycle and a security review. That process is rational and it is also slow, and for a market the size of mine it would have consumed more attention than the problem was worth. So the small markets wait, and in the meantime the work carries on being done by hand.

WHAT WOULD HAVE GOTTEN THROUGH

Know what you are actually competing with. It is not the other vendor. It is somebody inside the account who can now put a working version together in an afternoon for nothing, because the tooling got that cheap. That person exists in every enterprise now, including the ones that block AI on the laptops, and they already know exactly what the product needs to do. Make the smallest market worth your time, or they will build it themselves.

03 / WHAT I THINK

They do not work for the brand. They work for you.

I had 55 beauty advisors under me and I was responsible for their salaries, their commissions, and whether they were motivated enough to get through a shift. That last one is not a soft thing. If somebody on that floor is having a bad month their sales change, and that changes my numbers. You feel it inside a week.

It took me a while to understand what was actually going on. They were not working for the brand. Everybody loves the brand, that is why they applied. But nobody stays late for a logo. They work for the person in front of them, and if they do not like that person they will do exactly what the job description says and not one thing more. No matter how much they love the brand, they will not be able to work.

Which is why I do not think adoption is a product problem.

When I put the tracker in front of eighteen people, nobody had told them to use it. No mandate, no deadline, no manager checking. They used it because I had spent two years building the kind of relationship where if I said this will make your life easier, they believed me enough to try it once. The product then had to be good enough not to waste that. But the product is not what got them to open it the first time.

That is the part I think transfers. Forward deployed work means shipping to people who do not report to you, inside a company that is not yours, who already have a full job. You cannot mandate them. You can only be the person whose thing is worth trying. Every decision on that rollout came out of this: the home screen instead of the app store, the month of WhatsApp running alongside, refusing to set a cutover date. None of that is clever engineering. It is just what it costs to not burn the goodwill I already had.

QUESTIONS & CAVEATS

Your questions answered directly.

Written by me. These are the questions that actually come up when someone reads the site, including the uncomfortable ones about how small the numbers are and what the AI work actually is.

01. Why did you build this instead of using the tool you already had? +

Egypt was my account and it was growing, roughly 6x over about 18 months. The advisors were sending me their daily sales over WhatsApp and I was retyping them into Excel by hand.

We already licensed a field-sales platform. It worked for the large markets and not for mine. Loading a monthly target meant emailing the vendor's IT team and waiting for someone there to do it, the dashboard did not show what I needed, and the per-seat cost could not be justified for a market that size.

Building the replacement cost nothing but my own time, so there was no spend to approve and no case to make. Two days from the decision to a running site, with Supabase behind it, which I learned on this project.

It was a judgement call, not a brief. Nobody assigned it and nobody was waiting for it. I decided, as the person accountable for the numbers, that the licensed tool was the wrong fit here. Worth saying plainly, because being handed a project is a different thing.

02. What went wrong in the rollout? +

The signup screen, and I did not see it coming.

The build was not the hard part. The advisors work off their phones, so rather than ship a native app I had them add the site to their home screen from the browser. I piloted with three advisors and the sales supervisor for a week, took their feedback, fixed it, then launched to everyone.

Then people could not get in. Registering needed an email address, and not everyone had an email account they could actually get into, or had ever signed up for anything online.

I did not force the change. WhatsApp stayed on alongside the app for a month so anyone still stuck could send numbers the way they always had, and I reconciled the two channels by hand. People moved across as they got set up, not because a cutoff date made them. Nothing was broken. I had just never thought to check whether the people I built it for could reach the login screen.

03. How much of this is actually AI, and how much did AI write? +

Less AI in the products than the word implies, and more AI in the building than most people admit.

The tracker and the move-out generator contain no models. Homebase has an opt-in model path behind a flag (USE_LLM_SCOPE=true) and a deterministic template builder as its default, because a string template was sufficient.

I built with AI, in Cursor and Claude Code, and I would not have shipped any of it otherwise. What I will not claim is that AI did the thinking. It will not tell you what makes two rows the same event, what a supervisor needs on a dashboard, or what a field team on phones can and cannot do. You have to know that first, and then the tooling gets fast.

04. Eighteen advisors is a small number. +

It is, and I am not going to dress it up. What matters is that nobody was required to use it.

The tracker was never mandated. Eighteen advisors across Cairo, Sharm and Hurghada opened it daily anyway. A mandated tool measures compliance. An optional one that people open every day measures whether it was worth their time.

For scope rather than adoption: I was responsible for 55 advisors at Dolce & Gabbana and for account health across thirteen markets.

05. What have you not done? +

I have not shipped a machine learning model to production. I have not worked at engineering scale: the largest user base here is eighteen people and the largest dataset is a few thousand rows. I have not managed engineers. Homebase's compliance layer is specified in its README and listed as out of scope in that same README, so it is a spec and I have not built it.

What I have done is take a broken operational input, decide what a valid record is, and get a field team in another country to work the new way without being told to. That is the part I would defend.

On my own sites, nothing is broken right now. The move-out catalog could previously show "0 items available" instead of the real count. I listed it here while it was open and it is now fixed, reading 11. I leave resolved entries up rather than deleting them, because a list that only ever shows open problems tells you less than one that shows what happened to them.

What these numbers do not mean

  • The dashboard on this page is demo data: recreated in HTML with invented figures and invented names. No real advisor performance is published anywhere on this site.
  • January to March was backfilled: the app launched in April. The first three months of the 1,324-row window were loaded by me in SQL from existing records, not logged daily by advisors.
  • Removed, not rejected: the 29 were valid rows that collapsed against the dedup key. 1,324 in, 0 rejected. Nothing failed validation.
  • No cycle time measured: month-end turnaround improved and I never instrumented it, so I am not putting a figure on it.
  • 18 live, about 20 onboarded: two real numbers counting different things, not a rounding of one.
  • The honest move-out figure is 20 of 31: the sheet says 21 of 32 because a COUNTIF double-counts a synthetic bundle row.
